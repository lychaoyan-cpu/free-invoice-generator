How to write an invoice: 11 things every invoice must include

An invoice is a request for payment that doubles as a record of the sale. Writing one is simple: say who you are, who the client is, what you did, what it costs, and exactly when and how to pay you. Leave any of those out and you invite questions, delays, and awkward follow-up emails.

Below are the eleven elements that belong on every invoice, in the order they usually appear — plus how to handle numbering, tax, and payment terms. The 11 things every invoice must include

  1. The word “Invoice.” Label the document clearly at the top. It sounds obvious, but a document that just lists prices can be mistaken for a quote or a statement — and quotes don't get paid.
  2. A unique invoice number. Every invoice gets its own number, never reused. It's how you and your client refer to this exact bill in emails, and how a payment gets matched to the right job. See our guide to invoice numbers and how to format yours.
  3. The issue date. The date you send the invoice. Payment deadlines are counted from this date, so it anchors everything that follows.
  4. Your name and contact details. Your full name or registered business name, plus an address and email. This is who is legally asking for the money, and it tells the client where to direct questions.
  5. Your client's name and details. The person or company you're billing, spelled the way they spell it. For businesses, address it to the company (and, if you have one, a contact in their accounts team) — an invoice sent to the wrong entity often has to be reissued.
  6. A description of what you provided. One line per item or service: what it was, and when or over what period you delivered it. “Design work” is weak; “Homepage design, first draft delivered March 4” leaves nothing to dispute.
  7. Quantities and rates. Hours, days, units, or flat fees — show the maths. A quantity and a rate per line lets the client verify the total instead of taking it on faith.
  8. A subtotal, then any discount and tax. Show the subtotal first, subtract any discount, then add tax or VAT if it applies to you, each on its own line. The order matters because it shows exactly how the final figure was built.
  9. The total amount due. The single number the client must pay, in the invoice's currency, shown prominently. If everything above is the working, this is the answer — make it the easiest number on the page to find.
  10. A due date and payment terms. Don't just write a date: state the terms behind it, such as “Net 14 — due 14 days from the issue date.” A specific date beats a vague “due on receipt” for most project work, because both sides can plan around it.
  11. How to pay you. Bank transfer details, a payment link, or whatever method you accept, written on the invoice itself. Clients pay faster when they don't have to dig through old emails for your account number. A short thank-you and any reference — like the client's purchase order number — belong here too.

How should you number your invoices?

Start at 1 (or 1001, if you prefer not to advertise that it's your first) and count up by one each time: 0001, 0002, 0003. Sequential numbers are the easiest system to keep consistent, and consistency is the whole point — each number is used once, in order, with no duplicates. Some freelancers prefix the year (2026-014) or a client code, which is fine as long as the sequence stays unique. Whatever scheme you choose, pick it once and stick with it; changing formats mid-year creates the kind of gaps that make bookkeeping miserable.

How do you show tax and discounts?

Keep them visible and separate. Apply a discount to the subtotal first and show it as a negative line (“Returning-client discount — −$50”). Then, if you charge tax or VAT, calculate it on the discounted amount and show the rate you used (“VAT 20%”) next to the figure. Whether you must charge tax at all, which rate applies, and how it should be labelled all depend on where you and your client are based — the rules genuinely differ by country and by what you sell. This guide covers presentation, not tax law: if you're unsure about your obligations, a local accountant or your tax authority's website is the right source, not an invoice template.

What payment terms should you write?

Agree the terms before you start work, then repeat them on the invoice so there's no surprise. Short terms (Net 7 or Net 14) suit small jobs and new clients; Net 30 is a common convention with larger companies whose accounts departments pay on monthly cycles. If you'll charge for late payment, say so in your agreement up front rather than inventing a fee on the invoice — a line like “A late fee may apply to balances unpaid after the due date; see our agreement” is a reminder, not a threat. Keep the tone matter-of-fact: most late payments are admin, not malice.

Five common invoice mistakes

  • Sending it late. Invoice the day you deliver, not at the end of the month. Every day you wait is a day added to your payment date.
  • No due date. Without one, your invoice sits in a queue behind everyone else's dated bills.
  • Vague descriptions. “Consulting — $2,000” gets queried. Itemised lines get approved.
  • Arithmetic errors. A total that doesn't match its own lines undermines the whole document. Let software add it up, or double-check by hand.
  • Burying your payment details. If the client has to ask how to pay you, you've added a round trip — and days — to getting paid.

A 30-second pre-send checklist

  • Client's name and company spelled correctly?
  • Invoice number unique, and the date is today?
  • Every line item described, with quantities and rates?
  • Discount and tax on separate lines, total easy to find?
  • Due date stated, and payment details on the page?
