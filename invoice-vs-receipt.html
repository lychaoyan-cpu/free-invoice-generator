Invoice Basics

Invoice vs receipt: what's the difference?

An invoice asks for money. A receipt confirms the money arrived. That's the whole distinction — everything else follows from it. An invoice is sent before (or in expectation of) payment and sets out what's owed; a receipt is issued after payment and proves the transaction is settled.

The two documents look similar — both list items, prices, and a total — which is why they get confused. But they do opposite jobs in the life of a sale, and sending the wrong one at the wrong moment creates real friction: clients who think they've paid when they haven't, or records that show income you never actually received.

When do you send an invoice?

Whenever payment is expected after the document changes hands — which, for freelancers and service businesses, is most of the time. You finish a project, hit a milestone, or deliver goods on terms, and the invoice starts the payment clock. A proper invoice carries a unique number, both parties' details, itemised charges, the total due, a due date, and your payment details. If you're putting one together for the first time, our walkthrough of how to write an invoice covers each element, and the generator on this site will create an invoice online from the same checklist in about a minute.

One nuance: an invoice can be issued before the work is fully delivered — a deposit invoice for a percentage up front, for example — as long as it describes honestly what the payment is for. What it can never do is pretend to be evidence of payment. That job belongs to the receipt.

When do you send a receipt?

After the money is in. If a client pays your invoice by bank transfer, a short confirmation — “Received, thank you” — plus a receipt gives them a clean record for their own bookkeeping and expense claims. Retail-style sales skip the invoice entirely: the customer pays on the spot, and the receipt is the only document, doubling as proof of purchase for returns and warranties. If you sell both ways — some clients on terms, some paying immediately — you'll use both documents, just never interchangeably.

Can one document do both jobs?

Often, yes — with a label. A common practice is to mark a copy of the invoice “PAID”, with the payment date and method, once the balance lands. That paid invoice then functions as the receipt: it shows what was bought, what it cost, and that it's settled. This works well for small businesses because both parties already have the invoice on file. What doesn't work is the reverse: a receipt can't substitute for an invoice when a client needs to trigger a payment, because a receipt contains no due date, no terms, and nothing asking anyone to pay anything.

A quick way to remember it: an invoice is a question — “here is what you owe” — and a receipt is the answer — “and here is proof you paid it.” If no money has moved yet, you need the question, not the answer.

What happens if you mix them up?

  • Chasing a payment that already arrived. If your only record of a sale is a receipt you issued early, nothing in your files says “paid”, and a polite reminder can turn into an embarrassing one.
  • A client who can't pay you. Many accounts departments literally cannot process a document that doesn't look like an invoice — no number, no due date, no payment run.
  • Muddy records at tax time. Invoices show what you earned or are owed; receipts show what actually moved. Blurring them makes your own books harder to reconcile, whatever your local reporting rules are.
  • A buyer with no proof of purchase. Skip the receipt and your customer has nothing clean to file for expenses, returns, or a warranty claim.

The fix is simply sequence: invoice first, receipt when it's settled. Keep copies of both, ideally with filenames that carry the invoice number, and your paperwork will answer questions before anyone has to ask them.
