Every professional invoice has the same skeleton: your details, your client's details, an itemised list of charges, the total, and a due date. The names, addresses, and figures above are invented examples — swap in your own. Notice what the sample does not have: decorative clutter, a wall of terms and conditions, or five different fonts. A template earns its keep by being easy to read at a glance, because the person approving it is usually busy and slightly annoyed by paperwork.

What each field is for

  • “Invoice” and the invoice number. The title tells the reader what the document is; the number (here INV-0042) is its unique ID for filing, referencing in emails, and matching the payment when it lands. Numbers must never repeat — our guide to choosing an invoice number format covers the common schemes.
  • Issue date and due date. The issue date starts the clock; the due date is the deadline the client plans around. Both are written out in full so there's no day/month ambiguity between countries.
  • From and Bill to. Your business name and contact details, and the client's. For companies, bill the legal entity that hired you — an invoice made out to a trading name sometimes bounces in an accounts department.
  • Line items with quantity and rate. Each charge gets its own row, so the client can check the arithmetic instead of querying the whole bill. Vague one-line totals are the single most common cause of “could you just clarify…” replies.
  • Subtotal, discount, tax, total. Show the build-up: subtotal first, then any discount, then tax or VAT if it applies to your situation, each as its own line. Tax rules differ widely by place and by what you sell, so this template leaves the tax line for you to include and label according to your local requirements — if you're unsure, ask a local accountant rather than copying someone else's rate.
  • Notes and payment details. The terms (“Net 14”), how to pay, and anything the client needs to process payment — a purchase order number, a reference, your bank details. If paying you requires a separate email, the template has failed at its main job.

Three ways to use this template

  1. Copy it into a document or spreadsheet. Recreate the layout above in Word, Google Docs, or a spreadsheet, save it as your master copy, and duplicate it for each client. This is the classic approach for a freelancer who sends a few invoices a month.
  2. Print it blank. Some trades still hand over paper on the spot. The layout above prints cleanly — just leave the fields empty and write carefully.

When a static template stops being enough

Templates are honest tools, but they have friction that grows with volume. You type the totals yourself, so arithmetic slips in. You track the next invoice number in your head or a notebook, so duplicates happen. You copy last month's file and forget to change the date, the client name, or — worst of all — the bank details. And a spreadsheet invoice simply looks like a spreadsheet invoice, which matters more than it should when a new client is deciding how professional you are.

None of that is a reason to buy invoicing software with a monthly fee. It's a reason to use a generator for the mechanical parts — numbering, maths, formatting — while keeping this template as your reference for what a complete invoice contains. Template tips for freelancers

  • Save a pre-filled master. Your own details, payment info, and standard terms never change — type them once, and only edit the client and line items each time.
  • Keep the numbering boring. Sequential numbers, one sequence for everything, no restarting each month unless your format includes the month by design.
  • Keep a copy of every invoice you send. Save the PDF with a filename like INV-0042-northwind.pdf. Future you, doing taxes or chasing a payment, will be grateful.
  • Send a PDF, not an editable file. A PDF looks identical on every screen and can't be accidentally edited by the client's mail software — or by the client.
